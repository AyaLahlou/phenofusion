Blooming Insights: Deep Learning Reveals Key Climatic Drivers and Shifting Sensitivities of Phenology


Aya Lahlou1, Linnia Hawkins1, Pierre Gentine1
1Earth and Environmental Engineering, Columbia University, New York, NY, USA.
Abstract
Plant phenology, the timing of life cycle events such as leaf emergence and senescence, plays a crucial role in regulating ecosystem dynamics, carbon sequestration, and the water cycle. However, modeling these events is challenging due to the complexity of environmental drivers and the variability of phenological patterns across regions in response to climate and plant species, and traits. Here, we show that deep learning, specifically temporal transformers, combined with modern satellite observations of plant phenology, can accurately predict the timing of ecosystem phenology across the globe. The model achieves high predictive accuracy, with R² values near 1 across major plant functional types, including deciduous and evergreen trees. Notably, the model effectively captures the spatial variation of phenology and its interannual variability, which has been a long-standing challenge. The model, leveraging an interpretable attention mechanism[a][b], reveals the main drivers of phenology across climate and plant functional types. The model also highlights the critical role of memory effects, whereby past environmental conditions, particularly from the previous growing season, strongly influence current phenological events. Further, the model demonstrates that water availability has emerged as an increasingly significant factor regulating phenology globally, while the influence of temperature has declined over time. These results underscore the potential of deep learning as a powerful tool for forecasting ecological shifts in response to climate change. The study provides crucial insights into phenology globally and highlights the need for future research to expand these models across more ecosystems and finer spatial resolutions.
1. Introduction
Plant phenology, the study of the timing of plant life cycle events and their response to seasonal and interannual climate variations, is a cornerstone of ecosystem health and stability, as well as terrestrial carbon cycling1. Phenological events, such as flowering, leaf emergence, and senescence, extend beyond biological interest, playing a critical role in land-surface processes and land-atmosphere interactions2. These events significantly influence the dynamics of the Earth's carbon and water cycles, with the timing of leaf emergence directly affecting plant carbon uptake and, consequently, the global carbon cycle3. Moreover, changes in phenological patterns can modify plant transpiration rates, thereby impacting the hydrological cycle4,5. Phenological patterns, such as the timing of leaf-out, flowering, and senescence, are susceptible to environmental changes and serve as key indicators of climate change. Shifts in these events, such as earlier springs or delayed autumns, signal changes in temperature and precipitation regimes. For example, warmer temperatures can advance leaf emergence, affecting the length of the growing season and the dynamics of carbon and water cycles6,7​. Monitoring these shifts provides valuable insight into broader ecological changes and helps predict future climate impacts.
Accurately predicting leaf phenology is crucial in ecological research and practical applications where the timing of phenological events significantly impacts crop yields and forest productivity. For example, in agriculture, the correct prediction of leaf-out and other growth stages helps optimize planting, irrigation, and harvesting schedules, directly improving crop management and yields8,9. Similarly, in forestry, predicting the timing of leaf emergence and senescence is vital for managing forest resources and assessing ecosystem productivity. Accurate phenological models can help forecast forest growth and productivity under varying climatic conditions, which is important for sustainable forestry practices9,10. Despite its importance, modeling plant phenology in land-surface and Earth system models remains challenging due to the complexity of the processes involved and the incomplete understanding of the mechanistic basis of phenology11,12.
Traditional phenology models emphasizing temperature as the primary driver of phenological shifts are becoming less reliable due to climate change and extreme weather events. Studies show that increasing air temperatures have led to shifts in phenological seasons, with spring-summer seasons starting earlier and autumn-winter seasons delayed13.  Recent research highlights the importance of other environmental factors such as soil moisture, radiation, and photoperiod in regulating plant development 14,15. Extreme rainfall events have been found to have opposite effects on phenological development compared to accumulated precipitation16 , and various climate extremes have divergent impacts on the end of the growing season across different biomes and latitudes1. To improve accuracy, future phenology models should incorporate multiple climatic variables, account for extreme weather events, and consider species-specific responses.
Recent advances in phenological modeling have sought to address these challenges by integrating process-based approaches with empirical methods, leveraging high-resolution remote sensing data and advanced computational techniques 17,18. However, these models often struggle to accurately represent phenology across diverse climates and spatial scales, leading to significant biases in predictions 11,19. For example, some land-surface models (LSMs) report biases of up to two weeks for the start and end of the growing season20,21.
The emergence of machine learning techniques offers a promising avenue for improving phenology modeling by enabling the analysis of complex environmental time series and enhancing model interpretability through explainable AI techniques 1,18. These advances have the potential to significantly improve the accuracy of phenology predictions across different regions and plant functional types, particularly in capturing interannual variations and adapting to spatial variations in phenology 11,19.
Despite these advances, critical gaps remain in our understanding of the large-scale spatial and temporal patterns of leaf phenology drivers, especially in relation to environmental variables beyond temperature11. Addressing this gap is essential for improving the accuracy of phenology models and enhancing our ability to predict ecosystem and carbon cycle responses to climate change. This study aims to fill this gap by developing a model that leverages advanced machine learning techniques and modern remote sensing to predict phenology across different plant functional types and climatic regions. By analyzing the drivers of phenology at a global scale, this research provides novel insights into the complex interactions between environmental factors and plant phenology, with significant implications for climate change adaptation and ecosystem management.

2. Results[d] :[e]
2.1 Forecasting Performance
The m[f]odel exhibits high predictive ability across all plant functional types and climate regions (see Figure 1). For broadleaf deciduous trees (BDT), needleleaf deciduous trees (NDT), and needleleaf evergreen trees (NET), the model achieves an overall R² value of 0.99 and root mean square error (RMSE) of 0.01 with a minimal bias of 0.01, 0.02 and 0.01, respectively.  For broadleaf evergreen trees (BET) alone, the model achieves a R² value of 0.98, a RMSE of 0.02 and a bias value of 0.04. The model also demonstrated strong generalization across climates, as defined by the across-site metrics (taking the long-term mean at each pixel)[g], with an R² value near 1.00 and a RMSE approaching zero across all plant functional types.
The model performs consistently in terms of reproduction of the climatological (mean) seasonal cycles across the world and across plant functional types, with R² values of 0.98, 0.99, and 0.99 for BDT, NDT, NET, respectively, and 0.95 for BET. Importantly, the model shows relatively high performance in forecasting interannual anomalies, with R² values of 0.69 for BDT, 0.43 for NDT, 0.45 for BET, and 0.33 for NET (Figure 1). Predicting interannual variability has been a long-standing challenge for both statistical models or land-surface models, further demonstrating the transformer architecture's potential for sequence prediction in environmental applications and the effectiveness of our method in phenology prediction22.

2.2 Drivers of the Start of Season[i]
Given the performance of our model, we then turn to understanding the drivers of phenology using explainable AI (xAI). One major advantage of the transformer architecture is the so-called attention mechanism, which allows the model to focus on and weigh the importance of different variables in a sequence dynamically, enabling the model to capture contextual relationships more effectively, regardless of their temporal distance in the input. This provides strong interpretability on the drivers of phenology across time scales (Methods). The primary environmental drivers influencing the start of the growing season (SOS) varies by plant functional type and climatic regions, Figure 2. At high-mid latitudes, the SOS for broadleaf deciduous trees is predominantly influenced by water availability[j] and temperature.[k] Exceptions were observed in specific clusters, such as the southeastern United States, northwestern Europe, and Russia, where radiation played a more significant role.
For needleleaf evergreen trees, radiation and temperature were the main drivers of the SOS. Radiation had a particularly strong influence in Boreal Canada, whereas temperature is more influential in the humid northern and eastern coasts of the United States. In northern Europe and Russia, the SOS for NET[l] [m]is largely driven by temperature, with radiation becoming increasingly important further east.
In sub-Saharan Africa, the SOS for broadleaf deciduous trees is mainly impacted by radiation and temperature, both ab[n][o]ove and below the equator. A decrease in the influence of radiation is visible closer to the equator, where water availability becomes the primary driver of phenology, consistent with recent findings on the role of water table[p]. A similar pattern was observed in southern Africa, where coastal regions were more influenced by radiation, while water availability gained importance further inland.
In South America, radiation had a strong impact on the SOS for BDT along the northern coast of Brazil,[q] while water availability was more significant in the continent's interior.[r] Water availability was also identified as the primary driver of the SOS in Central America and southeastern Asia for broadleaf deciduous trees.
For broadleaf evergreen forests, t[s]he model indicated that temperature, radiation, and water availability had a near-equal influence on the SOS. In Australia, the SOS for broadleaf deciduous trees was primarily driven by radiation.[t] For needleleaf deciduous forests, t[u]emperature was the predominant driver, with water availability and radiation having equal secondary impacts (Figure x).[v]
2.3 Drivers of the End of Season
The environmental drivers influencing the end of the growing season (EOS) differ from those of the SOS, with water availability emerging as a dominant factor across multiple regions for the end of season. In sub-Saharan Africa, South America, and the southern and eastern coasts of Australia, water availability is the primary driver of the EOS. [w]Areas in northern Europe and Russia, where radiation is a key driver of the SOS, are primarily influenced by temperature and water availability during the EOS.[x]
For needleleaf evergreen forests in northern Europe and Russia, radiation still plays a significant role in determining the EOS.[y] The model suggested that for broadleaf deciduous forests, water availability was a strong driver of the EOS, while radiation and temperature were more critical during the SOS. In northern Australia, phenology was primarily driven by radiation and temperature for both SOS and EOS. In high-altitude regions of South Asia, radiation is the primary driver for both the start and end of the growing season (Figure x)[z].

2.4 Memory Effects
Given the interpretability of our deep learning model, we can assess the time lag for the influence of environmental drivers.[aa] We therefore use this to analyze the memory effects of different climate drivers on SOS and EOS. The model's attention scores highlighted the long-term dependencies between past and current seasons, particularly the impact of previous year’s spring precipitation on the length of the growing season in the following year (Figure 3 ). Specifically, we want to understand where the previous season had the most influence on the phenological cycle. This effect was most pronounced in broadleaf deciduous trees between 20° and 50°N, where winter conditions had a substantial impact on both SOS and EOS predictions. [ab]
Broadleaf deciduous trees in mid-high latitudes were n[ac]otably impacted by time steps from the winter season, with both SOS and EOS forecasts being strongly influenced by environmental conditions during this period.
2.5 Sensitivity Change (2002-2010)
Since our deep learning model is able to accurately predict the interannual variability across regions and plant functional types, we use it to understand the main changes in phenology across the world from different drivers of climate change. Between 1982 and [ad]2022, there were notable shifts in the sensitivity of phenology to environmental drivers. The impact of water availability on both SOS and EOS has generally increased, especially over Africa and Brazil. [ae]However, in southern Africa, there has been a decrease in sensitivity to water availability during the EOS, with radiation has become a more important factor. The influence of radiation on the EOS also increased in northern Europe and Russia.
In the southeastern United States, interesting patterns were observed in the drivers of the SOS, with an increased impact of radiation closer to the coast, and a decreased influence in states such as West Virginia, Tennessee, Kentucky, Michigan, and Arkansas, where water availability became more significant. Across all regions, the impact of temperature on both SOS and EOS appeared to decrease during this period (Figure x).[af]




3. Discussion[ag]
In this study, we developed a model capable of accurately forecasting phenology across four major plant functional types (PFTs): deciduous broadleaf trees (DBT), evergreen broadleaf trees (EBT), evergreen needleleaf forests (ENF), and boreal needleleaf forests (BNF). Our model demonstrated high predictive accuracy, achieving R² values of 0.99 for DBT, ENF, and BNF, and 0.98 for EBT. These results underscore the model's robustness across diverse seasonal cycles, with R² values of 0.98, 0.99, 0.95, and 0.99 for DBT, DNT (deciduous needleleaf trees), EBT, and ENT (evergreen needleleaf trees), respectively.[ah]
3.1 Enhanced Predictive Accuracy and Its Implications
The high predictive accuracy achieved by our model, particularly in forecasting interannual anomalies, represents a significant advancement over previous phenology models, which have often struggled with this aspect. The R² values of 0.69 for DBT, 0.45 for EBT, 0.43 for DNT, and 0.33 for ENT highlight the model's capability to capture phenological variability, though with varying degrees of success across different PFTs. The relatively lower performance in ENT could be attributed to the model's reliance on photoperiod as a key driver, a factor with minimal interannual variation[ai]. This challenge has been noted in other studies, such as Zhang et al. (2017), where photoperiod-based models showed limitations in capturing interannual phenological shifts . Our findings suggest that while temperature and precipitation are significant, their influence may be modula[aj]ted by other factors, especially in ecosystems where photoperiod plays a critical role.
3.2 Spatial Variation in Environmental Drivers
Our analysis revealed significant variation in the primary drivers of the start (SOS) and end (EOS) of the growing season across different regions and PFTs. In high-mid latitude regions, the SOS for broadleaf deciduous trees is predominantly influenced by temperature and water availability, consistent with previous findings by Zhang et al. (2017) and Piao et al. (2019) . The pronounced role of radiation in specific clusters, such as the southeastern United States and northern Europe, aligns with the findings of Fu et al. (2021), who emphasized the importance of solar radiation in determining phenological events in these regions. For needleleaf evergreen trees, radiation and temperature were identified as primary drivers, particularly in Boreal Canada, northern Europe, and western Russia, echoing the conclusions of Descals et al. (2022) regarding the significant impact of solar radiation on spring phenology at higher latitudes.
The shift in dominant drivers from SOS to EOS, particularly the increased importance of water availability, reflects the dynamic environmental conditions throughout the growing season. Studies like Liu et al. (2020) have shown that soil moisture and water availability become increasingly critical as the growing season progresses, particularly in water-limited regions such as sub-Saharan Africa and South America. This shift underscores the necessity of incorporating dynamic interactions between temperature, radiation, and water availability in phenology models.
3.3 Implications of Shifting Environmental Drivers
The increasing influence of water availability on both SOS and EOS, particularly in drought-prone regions such as Africa, South America, boreal Canada, and South Asia, suggests that areas under greater climatic stress exhibit stronger phenological responses to water availability. This trend is consistent with findings by Wang (2022), which indicated that phenological shifts in these regions are increasingly driven by changes in precipitation and soil moisture . Conversely, the model indicates a declining impact of temperature on SOS and EOS over time, aligning with recent findings suggest many regions may be reaching thermal [ak]thresholds where further warming no longer significantly advances phenological events 12.
These observations point to a broader shift in the relative importance of environmental drivers on phenology. As temperature effects stabilize, other factors such as water availability and radiation are gaining prominence in driving seasonal cycles. This highlights the need for future phenological models to account for the evolving significance of these environmental factors, particularly in the context of climate change.
3.4 Memory Effects and Long-Term Dependencies
Our analysis of memory effects revealed that most regions and PF[al]Ts are primarily influenced by the previous growing season's environmental conditions when forecasting the current season. This finding aligns with the concept of ecological memory, where the conditions of one season affect physiological processes in subsequent seasons 23. The observed long-term dependency, particularly the influence of spring precipitation on the subsequent year’s growing season, supports the findings of Chuine (2010) and Hänninen (2016), who identified winter dormancy as a critical period for predicting phenological timing 24,25.
The strong correlation between phenological events and past environmental conditions suggests that incorporating multivariate memory effects into phenology models can significantly enhance their predictive accuracy. Such models could provide more accurate forecasts of phenological shifts under changing climatic conditions, particularly in regions where water availability is a limiting factor.
3.5 Limitations and Future Directions
While our model demonstrates high predictive accuracy, several limitations warrant consideration. The reliance on remote sensing data introduces uncertainties, particularly due to the MODIS orbit shift, which can affect vegetation and land cover data accuracy . Additionally, the spatial resolution of the model, while suitable for broad-scale analysis, may not capture fine-scale variations in phenology, particularly in regions with heterogeneous landscapes or complex microclimates as well as differences across species or plants’ traits. Future studies could enhance the precision of such phenology models by integrating in-situ observations or drone data and applying them to smaller regions with higher resolution.
3.6 Broader Ecological Implications
The implications of our findings are significant for understanding how climate change may alter phenological patterns. As temperature and radiation continue to change under global warming, the timing of SOS and EOS may shift, potentially leading to longer or shorter growing seasons depending on the region and PFT. These shifts could have cascading effects on ecosystem productivity and carbon cycling, emphasizing the importance of refining phenology models to better predict these changes. Future models that can accurately incorporate these shifting environmental drivers will be crucial in managing ecosystems and mitigating the impacts of climate change.
5.. Conclusion
This study represents a significant advance in the field of phenology modeling, leveraging advanced deep learning techniques to achieve unprecedented predictive accuracy across diverse plant functional types. The model’s high predictive performance underscores its exceptional ability to predict across a wide range of regions and environmental conditions. Combined with its high interpretability, this method is a powerful tool for understanding and predicting complex phenological patterns.
One of the key contributions of this work is the integration of long-term memory effects into phenology forecasting. Our findings reveal that past environmental conditions, particularly in preceding seasons, can play a crucial role in shaping current phenological events. This novel approach allows for a more nuanced understanding of how temporal dependencies influence phenology, offering insights that were previously unattainable with traditional modeling methods.
The study also highlights significant regional and functional variability in the environmental drivers of phenological events. For example, in high-latitude regions, temperature and water availability are critical drivers, while in specific clusters, such as the southeastern United States, radiation emerges as a dominant factor. These findings emphasize the need for location-specific models that account for the unique environmental conditions influencing phenology in different regions and across different plant functional types.
Moreover, our analysis uncovered a shift in the sensitivity of phenology to environmental drivers over time. Water availability has become increasingly important, while the influence of temperature has waned. This trend is particularly evident in regions like sub-Saharan Africa and South America, where water availability now plays a pivotal role in determining the timing of phenological events. These results suggest that as climate change progresses, the traditional focus on temperature as the primary driver of phenology may need to be re-evaluated in favor of more complex models that account for a broader range of environmental factors.
While our model demonstrates strong predictive capabilities, it is important to acknowledge potential limitations. For instance, the reliance on coarse remote sensing data, while offering extensive spatial and temporal coverage, may introduce uncertainties due to resolution constraints or data quality. For instance, in dense tropical forests, microwave soil moisture retrievals are relatively poor. Additionally, the model’s performance, though robust, may vary in ecosystems with extreme or rapidly changing environmental conditions that might have been underrepresented in the training data. Future research should aim to address these limitations by incorporating higher-resolution data and expanding the range of ecosystems studied.
The findings of this study have significant implications, particularly within the context of climate change and ecosystem management. As environmental conditions continue to evolve, the capacity to accurately predict phenological patterns is essential for developing strategies to mitigate the impacts of global warming on biodiversity and ecosystem services. This study serves as proof that deep learning methods not only advance the field of phenology modeling but also provide crucial tools for understanding the complex interactions between vegetation and climate.
6. Methods
Datasets
Vegetation Index (CSIF Data):
To robustly track phenological shifts across various climates and time periods, we utilized solar-induced chlorophyll fluorescence (SIF) as a proxy for photosynthetic activity. Specifically, we employed the long-term Contiguous SIF dataset, a novel long-term contiguous SIF dataset with a temporal span from 1982 to 202126. This dataset leverages a neural network model trained on surface reflectance bands from the Advanced Very High Resolution Radiometer (AVHRR) and MODerate-resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS), to reproduce the Orbiting Carbon Observatory-2 (OCO-2) SIF data at long time scales.
The original LCSIF dataset has a spatial resolution of 0.05 degrees and a temporal resolution of 15 days. To match the spatial resolution of other climate variables and enhance computational efficiency, we downsampled the spatial resolution to 0.25 degrees. The temporal resolution was interpolated to daily time steps using a cubic spline function. This interpolation method was chosen because it provides a smooth transition between data points, which is crucial for maintaining the continuity and quality of the time series data at daily time scales.
ERA-5 Reanalysis Data:
For climate forcing, we used the ERA-5 reanalysis data from the Copernicus Climate Change Service (C3S) Climate Data Store (CDS)27. This dataset includes key environmental predictors such as maximum and minimum daily temperatures, soil moisture, precipitation, solar radiation, and photoperiod, all at a spatial resolution of 0.25 degrees. To ensure consistency with the LCSIF dataset, we performed spatial downscaling on the CSIF data to align it with the ERA-5 resolution.
Soil Texture Data:
Soil texture data were derived from the Noah model within the Global Land Data Assimilation System (GLDAS2), which uses the FAO 16-category soil texture classification28. The soil texture map combines 30-second STATSGO data over the contiguous United States (CONUS) with 5-minute FAO data for other regions. This static predictor was included to account for the influence of soil properties on phenological patterns.
Data Collection and Preparation:
The compiled dataset spans from 1982 to 2021, covering all continents except Antarctica, at a spatial resolution of 0.25 degrees. Due to the high variability of phenology across different species, we categorized the data by plant functional type (PFT): needleleaf evergreen trees, needleleaf deciduous trees, broadleaf deciduous trees, and broadleaf evergreen trees. Broadleaf deciduous trees were further divided into four latitudinal clusters: (1) 90-50°N, (2) 50-20°N, (3) 20°N-20°S, and (4) 20-60°S. For quality control, we selected only pixels where the land cover of the PFT of interest exceeded 50%. Before model training, we normalized all input features using z-score normalization to ensure comparability and stability during the training process.


Temporal transformer model
Data Splitting:
We temporally split the dataset, with 50% allocated to training, 25% to validation, and 25% to testing. The training set included data from 1982-1992 and 2011-2021, chosen to mitigate biases from non-stationary climate conditions and potential inaccuracies in older remote sensing data. The validation set and testing set covered the periods 1992-2002 and 2002-2010 respectively. We divided each time series into samples of 395 days, using the first 365 days as historical input and the remaining 30 days as the forecast period, where only environmental predictors were known.
Model Selection and Hyperparameter Tuning:
Initial attempts with a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) model yielded suboptimal results, particularly in forecasting across different seasonalities29.[am] We selected the Temporal Fusion Transformers (TFT) model due to its superior performance in multi-horizon forecasting, its ability to handle long-term dependencies, and its interpretability30. The TFT model performed better because of its ability to capture complex temporal relationships and interactions among input features, which are critical for accurate phenology predictions.
We performed hyperparameter tuning using a random search procedure, optimizing parameters such as batch size, the number of layers, the number of attention heads, and the learning rate. Early stopping was employed during training to prevent overfitting.
Evaluation Metrics:
We assessed model performance using root mean square error (RMSE), the coefficient of determination (R²), and bias values. The evaluation process involved decomposing observed and predicted data into three categories of variability: among-site variability, seasonal variation, and anomalies. We first calculated the mean seasonal cycle (FMSC) per site by averaging monthly values across all available years. To ensure robustness, we required at least two years of data for each month. We then calculated a mean value for each site (FSITE) and computed seasonal variation (FSEAS) by removing FSITE from FMSC. Anomalies were defined as deviations from FMSC. Performance metrics included Pearson’s correlation (Cor) or R squared, Nash-Sutcliffe’s modeling efficiency (MEf) or BIAS, RMSE. Extreme outliers, identified as data points outside the range of median residuals ±7 times the interquartile range, were removed to avoid biased statistics.


6.3 Global Mapping of Phenology Drivers and Legacy Effects
Feature Importance and Memory Effects:
We used the multi-horizon attention scores from the TFT model's interpretable multi-head attention mechanism to analyze feature importance and memory effects. The historical and future selection weights obtained from variable selection gates allowed us to determine the relative importance of environmental drivers at specific time steps.
Mapping Phenology Drivers:
To map the drivers of phenological events such as SOS and EOS, we identified the 30-day period with the highest attention score sum across the 365 historical time steps and 30 future time steps. We calculated the importance of temperature by summing the feature importance values for maximum and minimum daily temperatures, the importance of radiation by summing the values for accumulated solar radiation and photoperiod, and the importance of water availability by summing the values for precipitation and soil moisture. The global maps were generated at a spatial resolution of 0.25 degrees, ensuring consistency with other datasets.
Mapping Legacy Effects:
We created global maps to represent legacy effects on SOS and EOS by selecting samples with onset dates near the start of the season at corresponding latitudes. The memory effects were mapped using the 30-day period with the highest attention score sum. This approach was also applied to EOS mapping, highlighting the temporal dependencies of environmental drivers.
