Predicting vegetation phenology—the timing of critical plant lifecycle events such as leaf emergence and senescence—is essential for understanding ecosystem health, carbon cycling, and land-atmosphere interactions under climate change. Yet, despite significant advances in Earth system modeling, existing approaches still struggle with persistent inaccuracies, particularly under shifting climate conditions. Integrating recent breakthroughs in deep learning, particularly attention-based transformer architectures, offers a promising pathway to address these critical gaps, enabling a leap forward in our ability to model, interpret, and forecast phenological responses globally.


Land surface phenology (LSP) provides crucial insights into how ecosystems respond to ongoing climate variability and long-term change, directly influencing carbon uptake, water cycling, and biodiversity. Accurate modeling of LSP is thus vital not only for ecological research but also for improving the reliability of global land surface modules embedded in Earth System Models (ESMs), which currently underpin our understanding of terrestrial climate feedbacks. However, traditional phenology models incorporated within leading ESMs, such as the Community Land Model (CLM) and JULES, predominantly rely on simplified, temperature-driven heuristics, resulting in significant biases in capturing both spatial heterogeneity and temporal variability—particularly under scenarios involving increasingly extreme climate events and moisture variability (Peano et al. 2021; Richardson et al. 2012). Recent advances in machine learning (ML), including convolutional and recurrent neural networks, have enhanced phenological modeling, but have largely remained limited to regional studies or datasets constrained in either spatial extent or temporal depth (Ma et al. 2022; Sainte Fare Garnot et al. 2024). Consequently, there remains a critical need for an approach that comprehensively integrates multiple environmental drivers, provides global coverage, accurately captures long-term temporal dependencies, and offers interpretability to illuminate the underlying ecological processes shaping phenological dynamics.

Land Surface Phenology (LSP)—the study of the timing of vegetation life-cycle events such as leaf emergence and senescence—plays a critical role in shaping ecosystem dynamics, terrestrial carbon sequestration, and global water cycles (Richardson et al., 2012). Accurate prediction and understanding of phenological timing are foundational not only for ecological forecasting but also for effective management of agricultural systems, forest productivity, and climate adaptation strategies (Peano et al., 2021). Consequently, phenology modeling has become an integral component of contemporary Earth System Models (ESMs), influencing projections of carbon fluxes, hydrological cycles, and climate–vegetation feedbacks (Fu et al., 2022).
Traditional phenology modules within ESMs—such as those in the Community Land Model (CLM5.0) and JULES—largely rely on heuristic temperature-driven frameworks. However, these classical models often demonstrate persistent biases, notably in spring onset and autumn senescence timings, contributing to substantial uncertainty in carbon-cycle predictions and vegetation responses to climate extremes (Peano et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2020). Critically, the emphasis on temperature alone has increasingly proven inadequate as climate change alters precipitation regimes, increases extreme weather events, and shifts radiation availability, thereby complicating plant phenological responses globally (Zhao et al., 2017; Qiu et al., 2020).
Over the last decade, a growing body of literature has recognized these limitations and called for broader integration of multiple environmental drivers—including moisture availability, radiation, and photoperiod—into phenology modeling frameworks (Wang et al., 2022; Wadgymar et al., 2018). Statistical and machine learning (ML) approaches, notably Random Forests and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, have emerged as promising alternatives, particularly due to their capacity to capture non-linear interactions and handle large-scale, high-resolution datasets from remote sensing (Guan et al., 2023; Cao et al., 2021). Yet, these methods often fall short in providing comprehensive global-scale applications or interpretability regarding the dynamic interplay of climatic drivers across diverse ecosystems and temporal scales (Liu et al., 2020; Fu et al., 2022).
Recent advances in transformer-based deep learning architectures, which leverage attention mechanisms to effectively model complex temporal dependencies, have begun to transform environmental sequence modeling (Lim et al., 2019). Transformer models have already shown remarkable potential in local- and regional-scale phenology predictions, outperforming conventional ML methods by effectively handling irregular and noisy remote sensing data (Sainte Fare Garnot et al., 2024). Nevertheless, existing studies utilizing transformers in phenology modeling have largely remained region-specific, limited by short temporal durations, or lacked detailed exploration of climatic driver importance, temporal shifts in sensitivities, and legacy effects.
In this study, we address these critical gaps by developing a global-scale temporal transformer model trained on a comprehensive multi-decadal satellite-derived Solar-Induced Fluorescence (CSIF) dataset spanning nearly four decades (1982–2021). Our model integrates multiple environmental variables—temperature, radiation, photoperiod, and moisture availability—into a cohesive and interpretable analytical framework. Leveraging the interpretability offered by attention mechanisms, we explicitly quantify the evolving importance and sensitivity of different climatic drivers across spatial scales and temporal dimensions, highlighting previously overlooked legacy effects where past climatic conditions significantly influence current phenological states.
Specifically, this work advances current understanding and modeling capabilities in three crucial dimensions: (i) demonstrating clear predictive superiority and robustness over standard ESM phenology modules, (ii) unraveling novel insights into shifting climatic sensitivities over multiple decades, and (iii) proposing actionable pathways for integrating transformer-based, attention-informed phenology predictions into operational Earth System Modeling frameworks. Through comprehensive benchmarking against established ESM modules and elucidation of complex ecological processes, our approach significantly enhances predictive accuracy, interpretability, and applicability in global ecological forecasting and climate change adaptation strategies.

Plant phenology—the timing of seasonal life-cycle events such as leaf emergence, flowering, and senescence—serves as a fundamental indicator of ecosystem health, carbon dynamics, and vegetation responses to climate variability and change. Phenological timing influences critical land-atmosphere interactions, including carbon sequestration, evapotranspiration, and energy balance, thus playing a central role in regulating global biogeochemical cycles and climate feedback mechanisms. With climate change intensifying, shifts in phenology have become among the earliest and most pervasive ecological responses observed globally, leading to widespread changes in ecosystem functioning and biodiversity patterns. Consequently, accurate and reliable phenological predictions have emerged as an essential component for ecological forecasting, adaptive ecosystem management, and improving resilience to climate-driven disturbances.
Given the critical importance of phenological events, their representation within Earth System Models (ESMs)—which project future climate scenarios and ecosystem responses—is paramount. Phenology modules within widely-used land surface models (e.g., Community Land Model (CLM), Joint UK Land Environment Simulator (JULES), and Integrated Biosphere Simulator (IBIS)) have traditionally relied on simplified heuristic or temperature-centric formulations. However, recent evaluations have consistently demonstrated substantial limitations in these conventional approaches, including biases in the timing of leaf-out and senescence, poor representation of interannual variability, and considerable discrepancies across different models. These limitations not only reduce confidence in projected vegetation responses but also propagate uncertainties into broader climate predictions, significantly constraining our ability to anticipate ecological consequences of climate extremes.
Addressing these challenges requires models that accurately integrate multiple environmental drivers—beyond temperature alone—capturing diverse climatic interactions and ecosystem-specific sensitivities at both regional and global scales. Enhanced predictive frameworks that explicitly incorporate interannual climate variability, extreme weather events, and long-term climate trends are urgently needed. Such advances would substantially improve our capability to anticipate ecosystem trajectories, refine climate projections, and better inform global climate adaptation and mitigation strategies.

Current State of the Art & Gaps (Last ~10 Years)
Over the past decade, land surface phenology (LSP) modeling has evolved considerably, driven by growing recognition of its ecological importance and persistent limitations in traditional modeling frameworks. Historically, most phenology modules embedded within Earth System Models (ESMs)—including prominent examples such as the Community Land Model (CLM), Joint UK Land Environment Simulator (JULES), and Integrated Biosphere Simulator (IBIS)—have emphasized temperature-driven heuristic models to predict phenological transitions. Recent systematic evaluations, however, have highlighted significant biases and uncertainties within these traditional approaches. Specifically, classical models often inaccurately predict the onset and cessation of growing seasons, particularly in ecosystems characterized by strong water limitation or complex radiation regimes, resulting in timing discrepancies exceeding several weeks and substantial divergence among model predictions.
Recognizing these limitations, recent studies have increasingly focused on expanding the set of environmental variables incorporated into phenological models. Research over the past decade has underscored the critical importance of factors such as soil moisture, photoperiod, solar radiation, and extreme weather events in modulating phenological events. For example, recent global analyses have indicated that soil moisture exerts a primary influence on vegetation phenology in arid and semi-arid ecosystems, challenging the longstanding assumption of temperature as the singular dominant driver. Similarly, the role of photoperiod has emerged as vital for high-latitude and boreal ecosystems, where daylight duration helps safeguard plants against frost damage, thus influencing leaf-out timing irrespective of rising temperatures.
Parallel to these advances in process understanding, the application of statistical and machine learning (ML) approaches to LSP modeling has surged, reflecting broader trends within environmental modeling. ML techniques such as Random Forests, Gradient Boosted Trees, and Support Vector Machines have shown promise in capturing complex ecological relationships and improving regional phenology predictions. However, these models have often struggled with generalization across diverse ecosystems, especially given the inherent spatial heterogeneity and temporal complexity of phenological patterns. Additionally, traditional ML models have offered limited interpretability regarding the ecological and climatic drivers of phenological variability, restricting their utility for ecological insight and process-based improvements in ESM parameterizations.
More recently, deep learning approaches—particularly temporal modeling frameworks such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks and Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)—have begun to address these challenges by leveraging large-scale remote sensing datasets and explicitly modeling temporal dependencies. Yet, despite their predictive success at local or regional scales, these methods frequently encounter difficulties scaling to global extents and adequately capturing interannual variability, climate extremes, and shifts in long-term driver sensitivities.
Transformer-based models have emerged very recently as a promising advancement in temporal ecological modeling, particularly due to their sophisticated attention mechanisms capable of dynamically learning temporal dependencies and simultaneously enabling high interpretability. Recent transformer-based studies, however, have primarily been limited to localized or regional scales with restricted time spans, leaving crucial questions unanswered regarding their global applicability, robustness across diverse plant functional types, and suitability for integration with global-scale ESMs. Moreover, existing studies employing transformer architectures in phenology modeling have generally not investigated the evolution of climatic driver importance over multi-decadal periods—an essential perspective for anticipating ecosystem responses under future climate scenarios.
Thus, significant gaps remain: (i) there is an urgent need for global-scale, temporally explicit deep-learning phenology models capable of accurately capturing diverse climatic drivers; (ii) attention-based interpretability remains underutilized in understanding evolving ecological sensitivities over extended historical periods; and (iii) integration pathways connecting advanced ML approaches to operational phenology modules within Earth System Models remain critically underdeveloped. Addressing these gaps is essential for enhancing ecological forecasting capabilities, reducing uncertainty in climate predictions, and improving our understanding of ecosystem responses to global environmental change.

In this study, we directly address critical knowledge gaps identified in recent phenological modeling literature by developing a novel, global-scale phenological prediction framework utilizing advanced deep-learning techniques. Specifically, we introduce a temporal transformer model, trained on multi-decadal (1982–2021) remotely sensed contiguous solar-induced fluorescence (CSIF) datasets, to predict land surface phenology across diverse ecosystems globally. This represents a significant advancement over existing deep-learning-based phenological models, which have typically been limited to regional analyses, shorter time series, or less sophisticated temporal architectures.
Our approach offers three distinct innovations:
(i) Global-scale temporal transformer modeling:
 We employ transformer-based neural networks, leveraging their powerful capacity for sequence modeling and dynamic attention mechanisms, to accurately forecast phenological timing across diverse climatic regions and multiple plant functional types at global scales. This enables robust capture of complex temporal dependencies and climatic interactions, effectively overcoming prior scaling limitations of regional deep-learning studies.
(ii) Attention-based driver interpretability:
 Through explicit attention mechanisms integrated within our temporal transformer, we uniquely quantify and visualize the relative importance of multiple climate drivers—including temperature, soil moisture, solar radiation, and photoperiod—across space and through time. By systematically analyzing attention weights over a 40-year period, our study reveals significant temporal shifts in driver sensitivities, highlighting a notable long-term increase in the role of water availability and a concurrent decline in temperature’s relative influence. This innovative interpretive capability directly addresses recent calls in the literature for deeper ecological insights and improved mechanistic understanding of phenological drivers under climate change.
(iii) Robust benchmarking against traditional ESM phenology modules:
 We explicitly benchmark our transformer-based model predictions against conventional phenological modules from widely-used Earth System Models (e.g., CLM, JULES). Our analysis demonstrates substantial improvements in predictive accuracy, particularly in challenging contexts such as capturing interannual variability, legacy effects, and the impacts of extreme climatic events. Importantly, the attention-based interpretability provided by our model generates concrete, actionable insights for potential parameterization improvements in current and next-generation Earth system modeling frameworks.
Collectively, these contributions fill pivotal gaps identified in the recent phenology modeling literature, significantly advancing our predictive capabilities and ecological interpretability, and charting a clear integration pathway toward operational use in Earth System Models. Our findings thus not only deepen the fundamental scientific understanding of global phenology dynamics but also provide practical foundations for improved climate modeling and ecological forecasting under future environmental conditions.

Plant phenology—the timing of seasonal life-cycle events such as leaf emergence, flowering, and senescence—is a key regulator of ecosystem function and a fundamental component of global carbon and water cycles. Shifts in the timing of these events influence land–atmosphere exchanges of carbon, energy, and water, with direct consequences for climate feedbacks and ecosystem resilience. As the climate warms, phenological events are advancing or delaying across regions, triggering cascading impacts on biodiversity, biogeochemical processes, and the services ecosystems provide. Predicting these dynamics reliably has become central to ecological forecasting, land management, and improving the fidelity of climate projections.
Earth System Models (ESMs) are the primary tools used to anticipate how terrestrial ecosystems will respond to future climate change. Their land surface components, such as the Community Land Model (CLM), Joint UK Land Environment Simulator (JULES), and Integrated Biosphere Simulator (IBIS), simulate vegetation dynamics through phenology modules that determine the onset and end of the growing season. Most of these modules rely on simplified, temperature-driven or heuristic rules. However, extensive evaluations have revealed that these approaches frequently produce timing biases of several weeks, misrepresent interannual variability, and diverge across models, particularly in semi-arid regions and at high latitudes. These deficiencies propagate through predictions of vegetation productivity, carbon fluxes, and climate feedbacks, reducing confidence in model-based projections of ecosystem–climate interactions under future extremes.
Over the past decade, research has shown that temperature alone cannot explain the complexity of global phenological patterns. Soil moisture has emerged as a dominant control in arid and semi-arid ecosystems, while photoperiod strongly constrains boreal and high-latitude vegetation, preventing premature leaf-out despite warming. Solar radiation and weather extremes also modulate both spring onset and autumn senescence, creating nonlinear and region-specific responses. In parallel, data-driven approaches have risen in prominence. Statistical and machine learning (ML) methods, such as Random Forests and Gradient Boosted Trees, have demonstrated improved predictive skill in regional applications, yet they struggle to generalize across heterogeneous ecosystems and provide limited mechanistic insight for improving ESMs. Deep learning models, including Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks and Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), leverage long-term remote sensing observations to capture temporal dependencies in phenology, but their applications remain largely local, often fail to capture interannual anomalies or climate-driven legacy effects, and offer minimal interpretability.
Transformer-based architectures have recently emerged as a promising alternative for temporal ecological modeling. Their attention mechanisms allow models to capture long-range dependencies in environmental time series while attributing importance to specific drivers, offering an unprecedented combination of predictive power and interpretability. Early applications, such as regional phenology detection in the United States or the PhenoFormer model for temperate woody species, have demonstrated superior performance compared to both traditional ML and process-based models. However, these studies remain geographically and temporally constrained, do not examine multi-decadal shifts in climate sensitivities, and provide no direct pathway for informing and improving ESM phenology modules.
Here we present a global, interpretable deep-learning framework that overcomes these limitations. We develop a temporal transformer model trained on four decades (1982–2021) of contiguous solar-induced fluorescence (CSIF) observations to predict land surface phenology across all major plant functional types and climate zones. The model captures complex temporal dependencies and interactions among multiple climate drivers—temperature, soil moisture, radiation, and photoperiod—while providing attention-based attributions that reveal how the relative importance of these drivers varies across space and through time. Our results uncover multi-decadal shifts in climate sensitivities, including an increasing influence of water availability and a corresponding decline in temperature dominance, highlighting the role of legacy effects and evolving ecological responses under climate change. We further benchmark our predictions against phenology modules in widely used ESMs, demonstrating clear improvements in capturing interannual variability, climate extremes, and long-term trends, while producing outputs that can directly guide the refinement of next-generation phenology parameterizations.
By uniting global-scale predictive power, dynamic driver interpretability, and actionable insights for Earth System Models, this work establishes a complete framework for understanding and forecasting land surface phenology. It advances both the scientific foundation of global phenology research and the practical capacity for climate-informed ecosystem management in a rapidly changing world.
