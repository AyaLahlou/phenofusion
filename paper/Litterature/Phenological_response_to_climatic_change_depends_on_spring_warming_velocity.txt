Climatic change is dramatically altering phenology but generalities regarding tempo and mode of
response remain limited. Here we present a general model framework incorporating spring
temperature, velocity of spring warming, and species’ thermal requirements for predicting
phenological response to warming. A key prediction of this framework is that species active earlier in
the season and located in warmer regions where spring temperature velocity is lowest show strongest
sensitivity to climatic change and greatest advancement in response to warming. We test this
prediction using plant phenology datasets collected in the 1850s and 2010s. Our results strikingly
conﬁrm model predictions, showing that while temperature sensitivity is higher in regions with low
temperature velocity, the greatest realized change in phenological onset is northern areas where
warming rates have been fastest. Our framework offers enhanced utility for predicting phenological
sensitivity and responsiveness in temperate regions and across multiple plant species and potentially
other groups.

Macrophenological studies—those evaluating species responses over broad
regions and many taxa—often focus on how climatic shifts, especially
warming, are driving seasonal event timing, including plant leaf or ﬂower
emergence1–3. This rapidly growing body of work3,4 provides a foundation
for understanding the patterns of phenological responses across multiple
plant and other ectotherm species along environmental gradients5,6. Still, a
unifying model characterizing generalities and underlying processes
determining the tempo of phenological change across space and time has
remained elusive. A generalizable model that characterizes phenological
sensitivity to climate conditions across taxa and regions, as well as the rate,
magnitude, and species-related variation in phenology responses, would be
of particular value, enabling predictions of change in phenology under
future conditions. Such a framework should directly tie into physiological
processes that are known drivers of phenological response, yet remain
simple enough to not require extensive tuning across species, regions or time
periods. Such a model could help resolve what sometimes appear to be
incommensurate ﬁndings related to the same phenomena.
One reason a universal model has not yet emerged is related to
methodological issues. Individual studies evaluating phenological change
vary over time in observation method and metrics examined, impacting
ﬁndings7. These empirical challenges have hampered efforts to consistently
derive insights about how intrinsic (e.g., species traits), and extrinsic (e.g.,
environmental conditions) together determine phenological response now
and into the future. Further, while mechanistic models are often highly

predictive, most are taxonomically narrow, rarely extending to the landscape or broader scales (e.g.8,). A large gap thus remains between processoriented models tailored for few species and phenomenological models used
to understand how clades and communities respond to change9. Somewhere
between these extremes are models that capture essential processes governing phenological response without requiring extensive system knowledge and that can be used to make broad, testable predictions.
The onset of phenological events, such as leaf or ﬂowering emergence
in plants, is directly determined in many temperate regions by accumulated
warmth, often measured in growing degree days (GDD)8,10,11. Plants such as
spring ephemerals and many early leaﬁng and ﬂowering trees have low
GDD thresholds to initiate activity, while others require exposure to additional accumulated heat before phenological events occur8,12. Although
other factors such as winter chilling and daylength can play a role in shaping
timing of spring plant activity, spring temperature-related cues are the
dominant driver in temperate systems13, and for many species, the speciﬁc
amount of accumulated heat required to trigger a life cycle event has been
quantiﬁed as GDD thresholds (e.g.8,12,14,15,).
When species’ thermal requirements will be met is shaped by three key
factors: the climate context of the location, how much warmth the organism
requires to undergo a phenological transition, and how quickly a location
warms up in the spring. We propose that these factors interact in predictable
ways, supporting our ability to predict the timing and sequence of phenological events at a location. The climate context of a location—whether it is

How quickly a location warms up in the spring—and how this factor
interacts with the others—makes for a more complex and interesting story.
A major determinant of the rate of springtime GDD accumulation at a
location, and therefore how rapidly various species’ thermal requirements
will be reached, is how quickly a location warms up in the spring. We refer to
this as the velocity of spring temperature. Locations near moderating
inﬂuences such as large water bodies are likely to experience relatively warm
winter low temperatures and cooler late spring temperatures. Consequently,
these locations experience relatively slow spring warming velocities. Conversely, locations lacking moderating inﬂuences, as well as low-lying areas
where cold air pools, experience colder winter low temperatures and warmer
late spring temperatures, resulting in rapid spring temperature velocities.
Locations with low and high warming velocities can occur in both warm and
cool climate contexts. Here, we focus on interactions between spring temperature velocity and species GDD requirements, extending the work in
Wang et al. 16, who ﬁrst discussed the importance of spring temperature
velocity in the context of phenological sensitivity.
The interactions among spring temperature velocity, the amount of
accumulated warmth necessary to force a phenological transition such as
leaf-out (GDD requirements), and the overall climate context (cool or
warm) lead to different and predictable outcomes, as reﬂected in the model
framework shown in Fig. 1. In all climate contexts, locations experiencing
slow climate velocities (green lines) begin accumulating warmth earlier in
the season as a consequence of warmer winter low temperatures. However,
these locations accumulate warmth at a slower rate, due to cooler late spring
temperatures. In contrast, locations experiencing rapid spring temperature
velocities (orange lines) begin warmth accumulation later in the season, as a
result of low early-season temperatures, but accumulate warmth at a faster
rate once daily temperatures exceed the base temperature below which
organisms remain dormant17. In cooler climate contexts (Fig. 1A, C), more

time is required to reach the thermal accumulations that species require to

We propose that together, climate context and spring temperature
velocity shape the temporal sequence of species’ phenological activity. In
cooler contexts, species with lower thermal requirements occurring in
locations with slower temperature velocities may in fact undergo transitions
later in the year than species with higher thermal requirements that are
situated in locations with faster velocities (Fig. 1A, C). In warmer contexts,
the opposite is true, where species with lower thermal requirements in
locations with slow temperature velocities are likely to still undergo transitions ahead of those with higher accumulation requirements in locations
with higher velocities (Fig. 1B, D). A consequence of these interacting factors
is that warming climate conditions should differentially shift timing of
events in areas with different temperature velocities. The model framework
predicts that climate warming results in species occurring in areas with slow
spring temperature velocities to show greater advancements than those in
locations with higher velocities. This advancement should be particularly
noticeable in species with low GDD requirements, as slight changes in
warming for such species results in a greater temporal shift than for species
with larger warmth requirements.
We test the predictions of the model framework using historical and
contemporary phenology data, focusing on 18 common plant species, which
include trees, shrubs, and herbaceous species in Eastern Temperate Forests
in the USA that vary in their timing of leaf-out and ﬂowering (see Supplemental Table 1). The historical observations were assembled by Franklin
B. Hough18, who established what is believed to be the ﬁrst national effort to
report plant phenology in the USA19. The signiﬁcance of this historical
dataset is that it involves collections made over multiple years (1851–1859)
at sites along a latitudinal gradient. We augment this historical dataset with
contemporary observations from the USA National Phenology Network
and iNaturalist. We use these historical and contemporary phenology data
to test predictions of our model framework, in particular, that species’ GDD
requirements, spring temperature velocity and spring temperature context
(i.e., warmer vs. cooler) interact to determine spatial and temporal trends in
the timing of phenological transitions. Given our framework (Fig. 1), we

We also evaluate the rate of change in phenology between historical and

dominant driver of phenological response20,21. Recent work has demonstrated
that the Northeastern United States has warmed more than adjacent regions
due to warming sea surface temperature and a more persistent positive North
Atlantic Oscillation over the past century22, suggesting that changes in phenology may be stronger in this region than in the Southeast23,24. Testing our
simple framework within the context of differential responses to warming
conditions demonstrates the potential to generalize this framework across a
greater number of ectothermic species and geographic areas.

Results
Historical and contemporary sampling of the 18 species used here are well
represented across the Eastern USA, with especially strong coverage in the
Northeast (Fig. 2). In total, there were 1714 historical and 2391 contemporary ﬂowering onset metrics, and 1689 historical and 3179 contemporary leaf onset phenometrics, i.e measures of the timing of onset of
leaf-out and ﬂowering onset in plant species included in the study. Sampling
varied across years and species, with most intensive historical sampling
occurring in 1857 and the greatest contemporary sampling in 2022. The
18 species used here represent commonly observed species (e.g. Acer
rubrum, Liriodendron tulipifera, Sambucus canadensis, Prunus serotina,
Syringa vulgaris; see Supplemental 1 for full list).
Spring temperature velocity—how quickly a location warms up in the
spring—has a near-normal distribution and varies by a factor of 3 across the
region (95% range 2.75–7.54 units of temperature change per month)
suggesting ample variation in this measure across the study region. Correlation between distance to coast and velocity of temperature change was
weakly positive (r = 0.126) and velocity of temperature change varied with
latitude (r = 0.564). The linear model testing whether period (historical or
contemporary) had a strong effect on velocity revealed a small (0.28 °C)
though signiﬁcant decrease in velocity (i.e. slope) in the contemporary
observations across all latitudinal bands; this effect was strongest in the
Southeastern USA (p < 0.001, adjusted R2 = 0.34). Conversely, we found that

A generalizable model of species phenology response under
climate change
Our results strikingly show that including spring temperature velocity and
its interaction with species’ thermal requirements is critical for understanding the shape of onset response. In particular, we demonstrate that
warming in areas where spring temperature velocity is lowest has the most
dramatic impact on species undergoing phenological transitions earlier in
the season, shifting timing earlier. This relationship is reﬂected in the
position of the green circle relative to the orange circle in Fig. 1 panels A and
B. Reversal in the sequence of these two events between cooler and warmer
contexts is the result of early-season minimum temperatures: minimum
winter temperatures are higher in warmer contexts, enabling accumulation
of thermal units above a base temperature earlier in the year. In contrast,
while the reversal in timing of onset for species in locations with slow and
fast temperature velocities also occurs for taxa with higher GDD requirements (Fig. 1 panels C and D), the magnitude of shift is comparatively
smaller. For species with higher GDD requirements, individual GDD units
represent a smaller proportion of the full accumulation required to undergo
transitions than for species with lower thermal requirements.
This model framework encapsulates and extends results from multiple
empirical studies that have shown that plants with earlier phenological
timing in temperate regions have stronger sensitivity (or responsiveness) to
climatic changes than those undergoing transitions later in the season31–34.
Higher responsiveness exhibited by species undergoing transitions earlier in
the season has long been hypothesized to be due to greater shifts in spring
phenology per temperature forcing unit33,35. Here, we are able to test and
extend these ideas. First, as in other studies, our results demonstrate that

We conﬁrm another prediction from this framework: the impact of
spring temperature velocity on onset is dependent on temperature context,
with especially strong effects of slow spring velocity in warmer conditions.
Our ﬁndings reveal that onset for plants with low GDD requirements found
in warm, low spring velocity regions is about 15 days earlier than those
found in locations with a fast velocity (Table 1; Fig. 4). In contrast, the same
comparison for plants with high GDD requirements shows activity
approximately ten days earlier in slow velocity areas compared to fast spring
velocity areas in warm contexts. In cold contexts, we see the opposite trend,
of stronger responsiveness in high GDD requirement species. The patterns
emerging from this framework hold great value to shape continued longterm monitoring efforts such as that carried out by phenology networks
worldwide.
Our model framework and empirical results also relate to work
showing that under equal warming, lower-latitude populations advance
ﬂowering more dramatically than those at higher latitudes25. These authors
theorized that spring temperatures are higher and less variable in southern
latitudes compared to northern regions, thus reducing risk from adverse
conditions, allowing greater phenological variability and better ability to
track climatic change. Other studies have found different results, including
the opposite trends36 and that different temperate regions of the globe show
markedly different sensitivity37. Overall, the question of sensitivity across
latitude has remained unsettled.
We have reframed the question of sensitivity away from variability,
which often is ambiguously deﬁned, to spring temperature velocity, i.e. the
rate of warming over the spring season. In the context of the velocity of
warming, variability can be thought of as the residuals of the model ﬁt of
spring velocity. This aligns with how extreme events are deﬁned, e.g. conditions that are far outside the bounds of typical conditions during a deﬁned
period. In this formulation, the importance of extreme events represents one
axis of pressure on plants and how they phenologically respond38, and spring
warming velocity is another. Those different axes are likely intimately
connected, and may themselves interact and should be the target of further
study. Further, while there is a reasonably strong correlation between

latitude and spring temperature velocity, only ~30% of the variation in
velocity can be explained by latitude. Also, unlike25, we ﬁnd negligible differences in variation across latitudes (e.g. SD = 25.6 for latitudes between 31°
to 36° N and SD = 25.2 for 36° to 41° N). Finally, one impact of climate
change, based on our results, is that spring warming velocity, at least based
on the sites we have sampled, slightly decreased from the past to the present.
This decrease is the strongest in the southern portion of the USA, which
itself likely drives differential phenological sensitivity change over time. This
suggests that one driver for advancing phenology in southern regions is
faster shift rates in spring temperature velocity, rather than simply a static
aspect of differences across latitude. We argue that an explicit focus on
temperature velocity, which directly relates to GDD accumulation, provides
a stronger framework for understanding temperate species’ phenological
sensitivity.
The model framework we present is equally applicable to ﬂowering and
leaf-out timing and to understory and canopy plants. Often these various
aspects are not examined together, and in the few cases where comparisons
among phenophases and plant growth forms have been made, researchers
have argued for differential sensitivity (e.g.37.) related to phenological escape
in understory plants39. We argue that some of these differences in responsiveness may have to do with the differential GDD requirements for key life
history events. For example, Acer rubrum ﬂowers precede leaves, while
many other taxa leaf-out before ﬂowering. However, the impact of temperature velocity for distinct phenological events within species is likely to be
similar to interspecies responses across temperature contexts and GDD
requirements. We did not directly examine this question because while there
was a signiﬁcant interaction between species-speciﬁc timing of onset (e.g.
early, mid, or late) and phenological event (leaf out or ﬂowering), the
variables were moderately collinear. Further, while we were able to leverage
species with different growth forms, including two herbaceous spring
ephemerals and multiple understory shrub species, we could not independently ﬁt models related to growth form because all the herbaceous plants
underwent leaf-out and ﬂowering early in the season. However, we ﬁnd
strikingly similar responses for early-season species such as Sanguinaria
canadensis, a herbaceous spring ephemeral, and Acer rubrum, a large
canopy tree. This suggests our framework is suitable to use across taxa with
vastly different growth forms, although more work is needed.

Temperature windows across latitude and interpretations of
phenology change
One of the challenges with macrophenological studies is determining
temporal windows for analyses, as the absolute timing of an event varies by
latitude40. Here we have used known rates of change in onset over latitude to

Article

deﬁne appropriate temperature windows and verify these windows given
our onset data. While it is possible that adaptation has acted to shape
populations differentially in different regions, we expect species to have
relatively similar GDD requirements across space, although more work is
still needed to test this supposition41,42. Using an approach taking temporal
moving windows across latitudinal bands is helpful for drawing insights
about how phenology varies over space and time, allowing us to ﬁt omnibus
models including both latitude and temperature.
These choices and our overall framework allowed us to evaluate
whether phenology change from past to present was stronger in Northeastern USA, where climate shift has been shown to be particularly strong22,
than in the Southeast. This hypothesized geographic pattern is orthogonal to
environmental patterns, where warm contexts and low spring velocity
should lead to the strongest onset responses over time. We ﬁnd a slight trend
towards a stronger advancement in phenology in the Northeast, strongest
for early onset plants. We directly assessed whether this pattern is related to
differential warming and found much greater warming in the north than in
the Southeast. Thus, while plants in southern regions are likely to be more
sensitive to climatic warming and experiencing decreasing spring temperature velocity over time, they are experiencing warming to a much
smaller degree than plants in the north, which show the strongest realized
response over time.
Caveats associated with combining different phenology datasets
A strength of the approach here is multi-year temporal replication both in
the historical and contemporary dataset across latitudinal gradients in order
to examine trends in phenology. However, the different data collection
protocols and approaches used to calculate onset phenometrics among the
datasets has the potential to introduce error. Observer effort, as well as
differences in phenophase deﬁnitions among the programs and the ease
with which phenological status can be determined can affect the accuracy
and precision of these estimates. In particular, the historical dataset used
here, while providing yearly replication across latitude, lacks detailed
metadata about observer level of effort for determining onset, and what
exact phenophase, e.g., budburst or unfolding leaves or both were used to
deﬁne onset. To minimize the impacts of combining information derived
from disparate datasets, our focus here is on relative shifts in timing rather
than absolute comparisons or calculating rates of change.

Conclusions
We present a model framework reﬂecting the relationships among spring
temperature velocity and species’ thermal requirements to explain phenological onset sensitivity and responsiveness. We then conﬁrmed predictions
from this model using a unique dataset spanning the Eastern USA and
approximately two decades across a 175-year time period. Empirical
observations of onset timing for leaves and ﬂowers were consistent with
expectations under our model framework and captured variation in both
sensitivity and responsiveness across the study area. Because this model
framework is fundamentally grounded in growing degree day accumulations, it has the potential for applicability across additional species and
geographic areas. Further, we argue this simple framework can be extended.
For example, one might expect to see adapted responses to change, especially over long time periods such as the >150 years between our historical
and contemporary datasets43. While not addressed here, it may be that
strong selection in more northerly areas in Eastern USA where climate
change is happening fastest has already sculpted response, especially for
earliest onset species (those most sensitive to temperature) and for species
with shorter generation times. One such response would be to increase GDD
requirements, thus simultaneously lowering sensitivity and leading to later
start to activity, even if climate change is forcing earlier timing. The model
framework presented here might also be fused with recent work examining
short-term climatic variability and extreme weather to better understand
how such events impact phenology. Finally, while we have not addressed
species-speciﬁc adapted differences in base temperature, they are likely to
impact the shape of responses predicted here and will be important to

framework, along with unique data resources we have assembled, provides a
stronger basis for understanding underlying processes shaping phenology,
and thus support improved forecasting of future phenology change.

Materials and Methods
Assembling historical phenology data
Franklin Hough of the Smithsonian Institute coordinated historical phenology collection by volunteer observers across the Eastern USA (typically
one or two at each site) who reported a city, county, or post-ofﬁce location
and onset date. While there are hundreds of sites, the yearly replication for
most sites and species was low. We opted to select a set of species that had
both ﬂowering and leaf emergence data and where the taxonomy of the
species was unambiguous across historical and contemporary data sets. We
also only selected species where ﬁve out of nine years had at least ﬁve
observations per year for ﬂowering and leaf onset. In total,18 species met all
those criteria and were targets for the next steps (Supplemental Table 2 for
list of species). Of the 18 species, twelve were trees, four were shrubs, and two
herbaceous spring ephemerals. Data for onset were too sparse in 1851 for all
18 species; all other years (1852-1859) are represented. We digitized these
data by ﬁrst extracting tables from a PDF scan for the 18 species and
converting the tables into comma-separated value datasets using Nanonets
(https://nanonets.com/). Each conversion was carefully manually proofed
to correct artifacts. Next, we georeferenced the sites using Google’s geocoding services. Three historic sites could not be located and were thus
excluded. Cleaned ﬁles were saved and imported into R for further
processing.
Assembling contemporary phenology data
We assembled contemporary plant phenology data for the 18 target species
from two sources, The USA National Phenology Network (USA-NPN) and
iNaturalist. Both platforms leverage volunteer networks to report on the
presence of species. The USA-NPN places focus on recording phenological
status44, while iNaturalist volunteers capture digital media of species which
are then identiﬁed via a community process45. Most iNaturalist records can
be annotated to report the presence or absence of ﬂowers or fruits.
We accessed leaf and ﬂower status and abundance records using the
USA-NPN’s Phenology Observation Portal for 2010-2023 (USA-NPN,
2024)46. Data were ﬁltered in R for the following phenophases: ﬂowers or
ﬂower buds, open ﬂowers, pollen release, breaking leaf bud and increasing
leaf size. For each site, species and phenophase (leaf-out or ﬂowering), we
counted a number of presences and for site-year combinations with at least 3
presences, and used the date of ﬁrst presence as the best estimate of onset.
We did not estimate an onset for site years with fewer than three presences.
Some of the 18 species with historical phenology data were not well
represented in USA-NPN. For those ﬁve species, we collated iNaturalist
research grade (RG) observations from 2018-2023; years before 2018 were
too sparse to ﬁt phenometrics, which here, are measures of the timing of
onset for leaf out and ﬂowering plants included in the study using quantile
estimates (see below).
iNaturalist RG observations are community-collected digital image
vouchers of organisms in nature with species identiﬁcation consensus from
two or more users47. For Sanguinaria canadensis, enough records were
already annotated with ﬂowering state to download only records with
“ﬂowers present” already annotated, along with associated images. However, leaf presences still needed to be annotated. For the remaining four
species, we fetched the images associated with each iNaturalist record and
annotated both ﬂower and leaf presence or absence using ImageAnt, a tool
for expediting annotation tasks (https://gitlab.com/stuckyb/imageant). We
scored any presence of unfolded or unfolding leaves as a leaf presence and
any visible inﬂorescence as ﬂower presence; completely closed ﬂower buds
were scored as absences. In rare cases, images were difﬁcult to score for a
variety of reasons and we excluded these from the data set. We opted for this
conservative approach, as we use a presence-only approach to generating
phenometrics48. Once annotations were completed, we imported all
6

https://doi.org/10.1038/s43247-024-01807-8

metadata associated with images and annotations into R and removed
records with absence and “unknown” annotations for leaves and ﬂowers.
To ﬁt phenometrics to the iNaturalist observations, we ﬁrst aggregate
these observations into 55 km by 55 km equal area grid cells distributed
across eastern North America using the R packages rnaturalearth and sf 49,50.
Our choice of aggregation was based on trade-offs between data densities
needed to ﬁt phenometrics and spatial resolution, and which reﬂect best
practices for ﬁtting these types of models48. We then assigned a grid cell
identiﬁer to each iNaturalist record based on its location. We grouped data
by year, day of year, and grid cell identiﬁer. If a day had more than ﬁve
observations, we randomly selected ﬁve and removed the remaining records
for that day to reduce bias caused by unusually high observation effort
resulting from iNaturalist events such as the City Nature Challenge51. For
two tree species, we opted to estimate onset using a subset of dates (February-mid July) because we know leaf emergence occurs before these dates
across their range and including late-season leaf data may make estimation
of onset timing more challenging. Finally, we grouped data by grid cell and
year and removed any cell-year combinations with fewer than eight
observations and fewer than 5 distinct observation days, removing phenometrics with the highest uncertainties given48. We then used the quantile_ci
function in the phenesse package52 to calculate 5% mean ﬂowering and leaf
onset metrics and conﬁdence intervals using 500 bootstrapped samples. The
phenesse package provides a set of methods for accurately estimating different percentiles of a phenological distribution using used presence-only
data. After ﬁtting onset metrics for each species, we manually removed cases
where leaf and ﬂowering estimates were well outside known time ranges.
Finally, we excluded records occurring below 31°N or above 46°N latitude.
Because phenology sampling in the contemporary dataset is more
spatially extensive than in the historical dataset, we performed a ﬁltering step
to only include contemporary phenometric estimates within 50 km of historical estimates. We placed a 50 km buffer around the historical sampling
sites and used the union of those buffers to create a new polygon, and then
intersected the contemporary data that fell within that polygon. This step
reduced the number of phenometric estimates to 3403 historical and 5570
contemporary estimates (from a total of 7998 estimates prior to ﬁltering)
and helped to ensure that historical and contemporary locations analyzed
are within the same general area. Finally, we calculated the distance to the
coast for each site using the extract() function from the distancetocoast R
package (https://github.com/mdsumner/distancetocoast).
Assembling environmental data
We assembled monthly temperature data for each species by site-by-year
combination. We chose a monthly temporal resolution as that is what is
available for the 1851-1859 study period. However, this resolution captures
a reasonable estimate of seasonal temperature velocity. We note a strategic
decision regarding designation of time periods used in this study. In general,
a single time period is often used to calculate GDDs or mean temperatures,
e.g. “February-June”, even when focusing on broad-ranging taxa or multispecies studies across broad regions. In such cases, a negative correlation
between temperature and latitude is almost always strong. However, summarizing temperature across a broad time period, such as February-June,
may not be relevant for a low-latitude population that blooms in early
March because this range includes values in April through June, after the
event occurred. Conversely, early season temperatures, e.g. from February
or March, may not be relevant for populations blooming later in the season,
e.g. early June. The result is artiﬁcial strong gradients that may lead to
challenges with interpreting phenological responsiveness40.
To avoid this issue, we used a sliding window approach to deﬁne spring
time periods across latitudes used for determining climate context. The
choice of these bands is based on Hopkin’s Bioclimatic Law, which was
formulated for the Eastern USA and demonstrates that onset timing shifts
4-5 days with each degree latitude. Because of this general ecogeographic
rule, ﬂowering and leaf out generally occur in late March in the lowest
latitude range, mid-late April in the middle range, and early-mid May in the
highest latitude range. Given these dates, overall variation across this

gradient, and the monthly temporal resolution of our study, we opted to use
different spring months for each latitude band. For the 31° to 36°N latitude
band, we used a January-April temperature accumulation window; for the
36° and 41°N band, February-May; and for 41° to 46°N, March-June. We
then conﬁrmed whether our 5% onset phenometrics appropriately ﬁt these
temporal windows (see Results). The end result of using this sliding window
approach is that we match temporal windows for determining spring climatic context and velocity to the actual onset timing in each band. A
beneﬁcial consequence of using this approach is a reduction in the latitudetemperature correlation from r = -0.86 using a single time range from
February-June to r = 0.07 when using a sliding window approach with
different time ranges across latitudes.
We used two sources of monthly temperature values: 1) Berkeley Earth’s
High Resolution monthly dataset (0.25° x 0.25° latitude-longitude resolution)
from 1850-present53; and 2) PRISM data from 2010-2023, at ﬁner (4 km)
resolution. The Berkeley Earth High Resolution dataset produces an average
over the series per month and monthly deviations, so we assembled absolute
monthly temperature data for 1851-1859 by extracting these years, and
summing the average values and monthly deviations for relevant months and
years using functions in the terra package54 in R. For PRISM data, we used the
prism R package55, retrieving monthly means from 2010-2023. We next
assembled mean temperatures, temperature standard-deviation (SD), and
slopes of monthly temperature change, using the time periods designated to
the corresponding latitudinal window. We opted to calculate standard
deviation and slope because they both provide a metric of temperature change
over the season. These measures are highly correlated (r = 0.88), so we opted to
focus on slope as our key measure of velocity of temperature in this study.
Speciﬁcally, we calculated the rate of increase in spring temperature per month
as our measure of velocity. Next, we extracted site-level temperature means
and slopes for the Hough (1861) historical records and the USA-NPN contemporary observations deﬁned at site centroids. Because the iNaturalist
phenometrics were calculated across a 55 km x 55 km equal area grid, we
aggregated monthly temperature values within each of the grid regions using
functions in the sf and terra packages in R and then calculated monthly
temperature means and slopes as described above.
Assembling trait data
In this study, we focused on a single species trait: species mean timing of
ﬂowering or leaf out, which serves as a proxy for the amount of GDD
required for a phenological event to occur. We assigned a trait category to
each species by ordering mean timing for ﬂowering and leaf out per species
using the group_by and summarize functions in dplyr56 and then looking for
natural breaks between early events versus middle versus later events. We
examined two different possible scorings that did not have a substantial
impact on model results. The scoring for dates occurring before doy 105
were “early” (14 species-state combinations); dates between doy 105-115
were “mid” (10 species-state combinations); and dates after doy 115 were
“late” (12 species-state combinations).
Statistical modeling
First, we calculated Pearson’s correlation coefﬁcients between velocity of
spring temperature and distance to coast and velocity of spring temperature
and latitude to examine potential autocorrelations. We also tested whether
spring temperature context and velocity varies over time differentially in
different latitude bands where phenometrics were ﬁt. These models used
either mean spring temperature or velocity as a response and time period
(historical versus contemporary) and its interaction with latitude band as
predictors. For mean spring temperature models above, we used a single
range - from February to June - rather than our sliding window approach.
Next we ﬁt a single linear mixed effects model (LMM) to test our
predictions using the lme4 package57 in R where the response is day of year of
the onset of either ﬂowering or leaf out and predictors included mean spring
temperature, velocity, both deﬁned based on the sliding window approach
described above, as well as latitude and longitude as continuous ﬁxed effects
variables. We mean centered and scaled all continuous predictors using the
7

Article

https://doi.org/10.1038/s43247-024-01807-8

scale() function in R in order to compare effect sizes and improve model
ﬁtting. We also included “state” (ﬂowering or leaf out), “period” (historical
or contemporary), and “timing” (early, mid or late) as categorical ﬁxed
effects predictors. We included all two-way interactions for all variables
except for longitude. We also included a set of random effects, including a
random intercept for year, since we have replicated years across historical
and contemporary time periods. We also examined random slopes and
intercepts for each taxon, in particular random slopes for latitude, and
temperature. We explicitly ﬁt our model to examine correlations across
slopes and intercepts for each of these terms. We used the ‘bobyqa’ optimizer
for ﬁtting models and used the REML default setting to better estimate
variance components.
After ﬁtting the full model, we used the step() function in lmerTest58 to
perform stepwise backward elimination of variables. We checked variance
inﬂation factors (VIFs) of predictor variables and interactions using the vif()
function in the car package59 and removed one interaction term that was
leading to high variance inﬂation but that was not essential for downstream
interpretation or model use (e.g. the interaction between phenology state and
timing of events). After removing this term, we re-ran models and checked
for values of GVIF^(1/(2*Df)), a calculation of VIF that takes into account
degrees of freedom, and no squared values were >5 (highest value was 4.53).
Because species are not independent, we generated a phylogeny for the
18 species included in this study using the rtrees package60. All 18 species were
included in the Smith and Brown61 synthesis phylogeny. We then used the phyr
package62 to ﬁt a phylogenetic linear mixed model. Because phyr does not
support correlated random effects, we ﬁt models with non-correlated random
intercepts and slopes but otherwise with the same model formulation. We
examined the variance components of the results for the random effects,
especially the phylogenetic autocorrelation component and also examined
whether the inclusion of the phylogenetic random effects had an impact on
coefﬁcient estimates for the ﬁxed effects portion of the model. Residual diagnostics of the best ﬁtting model were performed in the DHARMa package63 in R
to identify outliers or nonlinearity, and we investigated for the presence of
signiﬁcant spatial autocorrelation in model residuals using the ‘spline.correlog’
function in the ncf package63 in R. Finally, we plotted model residuals latitude
and longitude to check for broadscale trends64.
Both because we have very limited taxon sampling of herbaceous
plants, and because iNaturalist phenometrics are at a more aggregated
spatial resolution than the other resources used here, we also ﬁt a set of
models for a reduced set of 13 species whose present-day phenometrics were
only from National Phenology Network, which also excludes herbaceous
species. We found effectively the same results in both the reduced and full set
analyses (please see Supplemental Table 4 and Fig. 4 & 5).
