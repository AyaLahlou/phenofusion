The timing of spring phenology represents a critical trade-off between growing season extension and frost risk avoidance, the balance of which governs
plant ﬁtness and shapes plant species’ distributions. However, few quantitative
assessments exist, indicating that this trade-off is largely untested empirically.
Here, we present a global meta-analysis encompassing 193 plant species across
126 study sites, ﬁnding consistently high freezing resistance (to −12 °C) with
remarkably low lethal frost risk (safety margin of 17 °C) during spring emergence. Under projected climate warming scenarios, predictions indicate that
advances in spring phenology do not affect the lethal frost risk under light and
moderate warming scenarios. This is likely due to the reduced temperature
sensitivity, consistent with trade-off predictions. By contrast, in the highwarming scenario, the freezing safety margin is predicted to expand (by
~1.7 °C), resulting in a lower lethal frost risk. This expansion may compensate
for the diminished freezing resistance predicted under extreme warming. Our
ﬁndings challenge the conventional view that simple environmental factors
moderate spring phenology, and highlight the necessity of incorporating
biotic factors (e.g., freezing resistance) and biotic processes (e.g., early growth
versus frost risk trade-offs) into next-generation phenological models to
enhance prediction accuracy under climate change.

Plant phenology, or the timing of recurrent biological events during
the year, plays a pivotal role in shaping species distributions, regulating water and nutrient cycles1–3. In recent decades, advanced spring
phenology with warmer springs is widely reported4,5. However, it
remains unclear whether this phenological advance, accompanied by

warmer temperatures, alters the risk of lethal frost (LFR). Theoretically,
spring phenology represents an adaptive compromise between maximizing the growing season and avoiding lethal frost damage, which
reﬂecting an evolutionary adaptation shaped by long-term selection
pressures6–10. The timing of spring phenology under climate warming

arises from adaptive trade-offs between two competing phenological
strategies. Emerging earlier provides a competitive advantage by
extending the photosynthetic period, maximizing light-use efﬁciency
and photosynthetic activity11,12, reducing herbivore/pathogen
pressure13,14, and providing priority access to ecological niches15, all of
which may enhance plant ﬁtness and reproductive success16. On the
other hand, potentially lethal spring frost events favor delayed emergence, which protect vulnerable tissues17 and enabling greater
resource allocation to growth and reproduction instead of frost
tolerance18,19 (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Table 1). Ultimately, natural
selection favors a balance between these two opposing pressures, the
beneﬁts of early emergence versus the risk of lethal frost events, which
allows individual species to optimize their spring phenology based on
local environmental conditions. This evolutionary balance, known as
the evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS), provides a theoretical framework for predicting plant phenological responses to a changing
climate.
Speciﬁcally, this balance manifests as a buffering mechanism:
safeguarding against lethal frost damage during spring phenological
stages while simultaneously maximizing individual ﬁtness. Such tradeoffs broadly shape plant evolutionary strategies and explain spring
phenological variation across species and regions18,20. Therefore, the
optimal phenological strategy for plants under climate change is to
maintain this evolutionary balance. Lenz et al.9 propose a metric
termed the ‘safety margin’, deﬁned as the difference between the daily
minimum temperature and freezing resistance during spring emergence, which operationalizes and quantiﬁes this evolutionary balance
(Fig. 1a). The key advantage of this dynamic metric lies in its incorporation of biotic factors (e.g., plant freezing resistance), rather than
relying solely on abiotic factors21–25. In fact, plants must cope with
stochastic freeze events throughout the year6. The capacity to withstand such events, termed freezing resistance, peaks during deep
physiological dormancy in winter and declines sharply with spring leaf
emergence7. Consequently, when both biotic and abiotic factors are
considered simultaneously, a larger safety margin indicates greater
tolerance of freezing (and lower frost risk), while narrower margins
correspond to greater vulnerability (Fig. 1a).
Climate-change-induced shifts in spring phenology may fundamentally alter this risk-reward trade-offs in natural plant
populations22,26, leading to three potential distinct risk scenarios
(Fig. 1b). In the high-risk scenario, earlier springs confer transient

competitive advantages, but a single extreme climatic event beyond a
critical thermal threshold could result in local extinction7 (Fig. 1b). The
low-risk scenario allows for near-complete frost avoidance, but comes
at the cost of a competitive disadvantage that may lead to replacement
by other aggressive species (Fig. 1b). The stable-risk scenario, which
best aligns with the EES, illustrates the fundamental trade-off between
advancing growth and avoiding lethal frost damage (Fig. 1b). However,
empirical data supporting the existence of a spring phenology riskreward trade-offs under warming conditions remain scarce, rendering
this theoretical framework highly speculative.
In this study, we investigate (i) plant spring freezing resistance and
associated safety margin, as well as their environmental drivers; (ii)
how warming-induced spring phenological shifts may modify riskreward trade-offs. To this end, we perform a global meta-analysis
encompassing 193 plant species belonging to 336 orders, 54 families,
and 122 genera across 126 sites (Fig. 2a, c). Data are collected from 88
peer-reviewed articles (Supplementary Data 1) published before September 1st, 2024 (Supplementary Fig. 1). The meta-analysis quantiﬁes
lethal frost risk during spring emergence and identiﬁes key environmental drivers predicting freezing resistance and the safety margin.
We also implement eight process-based phenological models to evaluate the trade-off between early emergence and frost risk under climate warming, as projected by three Shared Socioeconomic Pathways
(SSPs). We show that biotic factors and the trade-off mechanisms play
a critical role in regulating spring phenology.

critical for accurately predicting future spring phenology under climate warming. Although recent studies have highlighted the complex
relationship between phenology and freezing events24,25, quantifying
how spring phenology responds to frost risk dynamics remains challenging. Our global meta-analysis offers fresh insights into how phenological strategies balance the potential growth beneﬁts of early

key ﬁndings that enhance our understanding of how plant spring
phenology may respond to climate change. First, we found that plants
sustain enhanced freezing resistance during the spring emergence,
resulting in an exceptionally low lethal frost risk, which may have been
driven by the stress of extreme events. Second, although spring phenology advanced markedly under future warming, the lethal frost risk

We found that Tmax, DTR, and Tmin were key predictors of
freezing resistance; however, the linear mixed model showed no
signiﬁcant relationship with DTR. This discrepancy is likely because
distinct freezing-resistance strategies (e.g., freezing avoidance versus tolerance) entail different biochemical requirements6. Variations
in methods used to assess freezing resistance can lead to inconsistent results. For instance, in differential thermal analysis (DTA),
the rapid biophysical changes during metastable equilibrium may
compromise measurement reliability32. This underscores the need
for further research to validate and reconcile these methodological
discrepancies.
The correlation between freezing resistance and various climatic
factors, including temperature, evapotranspiration, and solar radiation, ﬁts within an eco-physiological framework. This framework posits
that the development of freezing resistance is shaped by the interplay
of intrinsic physiological constraints and extrinsic ecological constraints. In terms of intrinsic factors, plant biochemistry, particularly
the concentrations of non-structural carbohydrates (NSCs), such as
antifreeze proteins (AFPs) and soluble sugars, is essential for freezing
resistance. Notably, NSC concentrations in plant tissues are higher in
colder regions33 and also in areas with greater diurnal temperature
variation34. This physiological pattern offers a mechanistic explanation
for why both Tmean and DTR emerged as strong predictors of freezing
resistance in our analysis19,35. In addition, plant species inhabiting
colder regions and those with earlier leaf-out dates typically experience more severe environmental conditions, necessitating greater
freezing resistance7,22. This may explain the observed positive correlations between freezing resistance and both Tmin and Phe-Tmin.
Extrinsic factors inﬂuence freezing resistance primarily through cold
acclimation and de-acclimation processes. For example, non-lethal low
temperatures promote cold acclimation and enhance freezing resistance, whereas warmer spells trigger de-acclimation, reducing freezing

resistance6,36. This explains why Tmax was identiﬁed as a key predictor
of freezing resistance in our study. We also observed positive correlations between freezing resistance and both evapotranspiration and
solar radiation. The evapotranspiration relationship likely reﬂects a
physiological trade-off, higher transpiration rates can increase tissue
water content and metabolic activity, thereby reducing freezing
resistance19. Similarly, under high light conditions, plants preferentially
allocate more resources to growth versus defense mechanisms37,38,
potentially explaining the negative effect of solar radiation on freezing
resistance.
The above discussion outlines the predictors of freezing resistance identiﬁed in our global meta-analysis, as well as the relative
importance of these predictors and underlying mechanisms. Our
ﬁndings align with established eco-physiological principles concerning
freezing resistance (Supplementary Table 6). Interestingly, we found
that the relative importance of predictors varied across both ecosystem types and species with different phenological strategies. Among
forest-dwelling species, solar radiation was a stronger predictor of
freezing resistance than Tmax, likely due to their enhanced capacity for
NSC synthesis and storage39. Conversely, grassland herbs face greater
water limitations40, particularly at higher elevations, their shallow root
systems must contend with frozen spring soils, which exacerbates
hydrological constraints41 and impedes the development of freezing
resistance. Plant phenological strategy further moderates these
adaptive responses. Early-spring emerging species with shallow root
systems must acquire resources (e.g., solar radiation and water) before
canopy closure11,13,42,43. In contrast, mid- and late-season species, which
preferentially invest in nutrient stores, rely on high storage capacity
that is determined by peak photosynthetic activity, a process regulated
by Tmax9,13.
Our meta-analysis demonstrates that enhanced freezing resistance signiﬁcantly reduces lethal frost risk (Fig. 5b). This indicates that
freezing tolerance is a primary mechanism for withstanding spring
frost, as opposed to simple phenological escape11,13,42. Furthermore, we
identiﬁed Tmean, Phe-Tmin, and FRS as key determinants of the freezing
safety margin. This is critical because narrower safety margins, which
are associated with a higher lethal frost risk, predominantly occur in
cold regions characterized by more frequent and intense frost
events7,9,20. Frequent frost exposure can reduce photosynthetic
productivity24, potentially undermining the biochemical foundation
(photosynthates, such as sugars, proteins, enzymes, and other nonstructural carbohydrates) necessary for freezing resistance. This

Our phenological models consistently showed that (i) climate
warming signiﬁcantly advanced spring phenology, though with
diminishing sensitivity (days per degree Celsius), and (ii) safety margins remained stable under low- and medium-warming scenarios but
increased signiﬁcantly under high-warming scenarios. Our ﬁndings of
diminishing phenological sensitivity align with previous long-term
observational studies43,44. As expected from risk-reward trade-offs,
safety margins remained stable under the low- and medium-warming
scenarios. Although Tmin across our study sites responded more
rapidly to warming than Tmax or Tmean (Supplementary Fig. 6), which
substantially reduces future lethal frost risk and should theoretically
support phenological advances. However, our analysis did not detect
such shifts. Compared to low-to-moderate warming scenarios, plants
under high-warming scenarios adopted more conservative strategies,
which substantially increased safety margins despite the longer
growing season. This paradoxical pattern will require further study to
determine its underlying mechanisms. Here, we attempt to explain
these ﬁndings within the trade-off framework. Contrary to common
assumption, plants initiate spring phenology depend on multimodal
environmental cues rather than relying solely on temperature45. An
overreliance on thermal signals risks a premature start to the growing
season, the well-documented ‘false spring’ phenomenon46. Consequently, by integrating supplementary cues such as photoperiod,
plants can achieve greater phenological precision and thereby maximize frost avoidance45. Recent studies have documented substantial
phenological plasticity, enabling plants to track ﬂuctuating temperature regimes2,21,45,47,48. Together with the reliance on multimodal cues,
this plasticity provides the substrate upon which natural selection can
optimize individual responses to contemporary climate. This evolutionary framework is precisely what our stable-risk scenario represents. Therefore, both photoperiod constraints and the reduced
phenological sensitivity resulting from insufﬁcient chilling44,49 may
represent adaptations that enable plants to optimize growth while
minimizing lethal frost risk. However, this system seems to fall apart
under high-warming scenarios (e.g., SSP5-8.5). One possible mechanism, a decline in freezing resistance under extreme warming (e.g., SSP8.5), but currently lacks robust evidence. To our knowledge, only two
studies to date have directly demonstrated that warming reduces
freezing resistance, with the effect being contingent on the magnitude
of warming50,51. For instance, Ettinger et al. (2019) showed that the
stalling effects of chilling appear only above 4 °C of warming30, a
pattern that aligns with our ﬁndings. A universal spring risk-reward
trade-off would imply that wider safety margins can offset declining
freezing resistance under high-warming scenarios, a pivotal hypothesis
that now demands empirical testing.
Understanding how spring phenology is shaped by the lethal
frost risk is critical for predicting ecosystem-level responses to global
warming. Our comprehensive meta-analysis revealed that conservative phenological strategies systematically minimized the lethal
frost risk during the spring phenology. Furthermore, models projections conﬁrm the central role of spring risk-reward trade-offs in
shaping phenological shifts under climate warming. Crucially, we
found that biotic factors, such as freezing resistance, are key regulators of plant phenology. This ﬁnding underscores the necessity of
incorporating biotic factors into next-generation phenological
models, as current models primarily account for environmental
drivers alone.

Caveats, limitations, and implications
Our study represents a signiﬁcant step into the trade-off between
spring phenology and lethal frost risk, providing a fresh perspective on
predicting future spring phenology. Nevertheless, several limitations
must be acknowledged. First, plant phenology and freezing resistance
data remain scarce for regions such as Africa, the Eurasian Steppe, and
the Southern Hemisphere (Fig. 1a), limiting our ability to generate
robust global predictions of terrestrial plant phenology under future
climate warming scenarios. Second, fundamental challenges in modeling spring phenology persist due to two key limitations: (1) discontinuous phenological series can undermine the accuracy of future
phenological predictions; (2) overly empirical parameterizations in
current phenological models31. Third, and most importantly, there is a
notable lack of data on how warming affects plant freezing resistance,
especially in trees under extreme warming (e.g., SSP5-8.5 scenario), as
implementing simulated warming on trees is particularly challenging.
Addressing such constraints will require innovative approaches. Promising directions such as long-term monitoring of freezing resistance
dynamics, which integrate controlled manipulations with advanced
physiological measurements.

Methods
Data compilation
In this study, we present a global meta-analysis of plant freezing
resistance and its relationship to lethal frost risk in the context of
spring phenology. We searched for journal articles on the ISI Web of
Science and the China National Knowledge Infrastructure using the
following key word combinations: ‘freezing resistance*frost resistance*cold resistance’ OR ‘freezing tolerance*frost tolerance*cold tolerance’ OR ‘freezing hardiness*frost hardiness*cold hardiness’ OR
‘hardening*de-hardening’ OR ‘cold acclimation*cold de-acclimation*cold re-acclimation’ AND ‘leaf*foliage*bud*‘. We selected studies
that satisﬁed the following criteria: (i) data were collected at the species level under natural conditions, excluding garden or glasshouse
studies to avoid human management; (ii) freezing tests were conducted during spring phenology on new leaves/buds using standardized methods (Supplementary Fig. 1), excluding studies based solely
on visual scores; and (iii) the study site location was clearly documented. For experimental studies, our meta-analysis included only
data from control groups. Means, standard errors and/or standard
deviations, and sample sizes were extracted from the study text,
tables, and/or ﬁgures using the GetData (V. 2.26) image tool. To ensure
the independence of data from individual studies, we averaged
freezing resistance measurements for each species within a study site.
In the ﬁnal dataset (Supplementary Data 1), the selected studies
spanned 110.53 ° of latitude (from 65.45 °N to 54.06 °S), 1 °-170.23 ° of
absolute longitude (Supplementary Table 2), and almost all terrestrial
biomes (Fig. 2b).

A central objective of this study is to project the future risk of
lethal frost. This is achieved by integrating projected shifts in spring
phenology and the potential changes in freezing resistance under
warming scenarios. Such an integrated approach is critical, as the
direct impact of climate warming on freezing resistance itself remains
poorly understood. To evaluate the freezing resistance during spring
phenology under warming conditions, we selected 15 perennial herbaceous species at the Qinghai Haibei National Field Research Station
of Alpine Grassland Ecosystems (37 °29′-37 °45′N, 101 °12′-101 °23′E,
3200 m a.s.l., see our previous studies for site details54–56). The warming treatment increased air temperature by an average of approximately 1.8 °C over a ten-year period. Plant cutting samples (leaves)
were cooled to each target temperature (using commercial freezers)
and subsequently thawed at a rate of approximately 6 °C h-1 until room
temperature was reached (Supplementary Fig. 11a). Initial assessment
of freezing damage was conducted through visual inspection, such
damage typically manifested as tissue discoloration due to polyphenol
oxidation, or as a characteristic odor resulting from protoplast
decompartmentalization and autolysis9. Following visual inspection,
cutting samples were transferred to small beakers containing distilled
water for electrical conductivity (Ec) measurement. These Ec assays
were conducted hourly until values stabilized, a process typically
requiring over ten hours (the slow leakage of electrolyte). Concurrently, fresh samples were boiled at 100 °C for 30 min and then
cooled to room temperature to determine the maximum electrolyte
conductance (Ecmax). In this study, freezing resistance was deﬁned as
the lethal temperature (LT50, °C) at which electrolyte leakage reached
50% of ECmax57–59, and this LT50 value serves as a common proxy for the
temperature at which 50% of the sample cells died9 (Supplementary
Fig. 11b).

Statistical analysis
We obtained a species-level phylogeny using the V.PHYLOMAKER
package16, which utilizes the largest dated phylogeny for vascular
plants (GBOTB.extended.tre). Next, we assessed whether evolutionary
history was a key predictor of freezing resistance and the safety margin
using Blomberg’s K (R package ‘phytools’, version 4.1.3)67. Blomberg’s
K is a statistical metric that quantiﬁes the phylogenetic signal in species
traits by comparing their observed variance against a Brownian motion
model of evolution:
Blomberg's K =

concordance between trait and phylogenetic relationships
concordance expected under a Brownian motion model

ð15Þ
Under this model, a value of K > 1 indicates that closely related
species are more similar than expected under Brownian motion,
reﬂecting a strong phylogenetic signal. Conversely, a value of
K < 1 signiﬁes that trait variation is more random than expected,
making the pattern indistinguishable from randomness and demonstrating a weak or absent phylogenetic signal. However, the K value
alone is insufﬁcient and must be accompanied by a signiﬁcance test to
determine if it deviates from random expectation. We therefore
employed a permutation test (n = 5000) to generate a null distribution
of K values (Fig. 2d, e). The observed K was compared against this
distribution, with signiﬁcance assessed at 0.1 level, a more lenient
threshold applied consistently across all subsequent analyses in this
study. We applied convergent cross mapping (CCM)68, an algorithm
designed to detect causal relationships, to explore the potential
causality between freezing resistance and the safety margin. The
method is grounded in the principle that if X causally inﬂuences Y, then
the state of X can be reconstructed from the historical record of Y,
because the evolutionary trajectory of Y embeds information about
X69,70. We performed the analysis using the ‘rEDM’ package in R. We
tested library sizes from 31 to 304 (τ = 1) to evaluate the convergence
of prediction correlation (Rho). The optimal embedding dimension
(E = 5) was determined via simplex projection over a range of dimensions (E = 1-20) (Supplementary Fig. 2). We assessed convergence using
the twin-surrogate method with 10 bootstrapped iterations, and considered a causal relationship signiﬁcant at 0.1 level.

To assess the relative importance of our study predictors, we used
random forest models as implemented in R (R package ‘randomForest’, version 4.7-1.2)73. Variable importance was quantiﬁed using the
percent increase in the mean squared error (%IncMSE), with higher
values indicating greater contributions. Negative %IncMSE values
indicate improved model predictions when excluding the predictor in
question, suggesting that the predictor should be removed from the
model. Many ecological processes covary, and the sheer number of
possible combinations (of predictors) in our analyses made predictor
ranking and the interpretation of signiﬁcant interactions complicated,
while also increasing the risk of model over-ﬁtting33,74. Thus, we
retained all predictor variables (without implementing stepwise
backward elimination) in the following analyses, as most explanatory
variables were statistically signiﬁcant (p < 0.1) (Supplementary
Tables 4, 5). Next, we evaluated associations between the two response
variables (i.e., freezing resistance and the freezing safety margin) and
seventeen predictors (i.e., ﬁve measures of site spatial distribution,
eight temperature-related traits, three moisture-related traits, and one
biotic trait (freezing resistance) using linear mixed models, with random intercepts among species and sites. To interpret the mixed model
results, instead of signiﬁcance testing with an arbitrary p-value cutoff,
we implemented the gradual language of evidence recommended by
Muff et al.75 (Supplementary Tables 4, 5, 6, 7), this approach allowed us
to communicate model ﬁndings in a more nuanced way16.
To determine the phenological temperature sensitivity (that is,
days per °C), we used ordinary least squares linear regression40 for the
entire future period (2020–2099). We then compared future temperature sensitivity to predicted trends in spring phenology and
freezing safety margins under future warming scenarios76. To assess
differences in spring phenology and freezing safety margins among
diverse warming scenarios, we applied linear mixed model with random intercepts among species and sites. We conducted all our statistical analyses using MATLAB R2022b and R 4.4.277.
